### Review

This is Rand at her most Rand, for better and for worse. Much of the book is spent reiterating that most works are plotless and valueless or value-destroying. She says:

> "The composite picture of man that emerges from the art of our time is the gigantic figure of an aborted embryo whose limbs suggest a vaguely anthropoid shape, who twists his upper extremity in a frantic quest for a light that cannot penetrate its empty sockets, who emits inarticulate sounds resembling snarls and moans, who crawls through a bloody muck, red froth dripping from his jaws, and struggles to throw the froth at his own non-existent face, who pauses periodically and, lifting the stumps of his arms, screams in abysmal terror at the universe at large."

This no doubt would also be her description of the people one encounters scrolling social media. I take her point that most art is of low quality, but I cannot relate to her level of horror at people, and those I have known in my life who exaggerate the scale of evil in this way are the most addicted and the most myopic and the most isolated people. It has always benefited me to see people as basically good, and that assessment extends to artists, even the ones whose works I don't observe because I think it's rubbish. But this is where I find it important that this book is titled The Romantic *Manifesto*. This is Rand being poetic in the way that appealed to her. The basic observations of the book which are summarized in OPAR and most soberly related in Chapter 5 of The Romantic Manifesto are sound. If one tries to take Rand's poetics about naturalism too seriously, they will be desperate for examples to back up her fervor, but upon reflection I am glad she didn't go to the trouble of attempting to put examples to it, because it would have made all the dullest parts of the book longer. As usual her core observation agrees with my intuition: The most interesting stories are the ones where things happen in interrelated ways via a plot structure, and everything hinges on the choices and actions of the characters. We read fiction to see how reality plays out. And indeed, I have read very little fiction that truly elevated my life experience. In this respect, Rand is vindicated in saying that most of it is rubbish.

This book gives me more respect for artists because it suggests to me that good art is very, very, difficult to make, and that it is the most sacred of pursuits. I only wish Rand's criticism of the state of the arts could have been more constructive.
